# Sweep 17A — INCIDENCE, not speed: what made games end in a kill elsewhere

**Aimed at:** the 2026-08-09 corpus measurement that **74.4% of the core-kill wins we
already achieve land inside round 250, at every rating band** — so the PROGRAMME's
`KILL_WINDOW_RND: 250` is **not our binding constraint.** The scarce quantity is
**INCIDENCE**: whether a game ends in a core kill at all. Separately: **353 games
reached r1000 and we won 57.2% of those on tiebreak**, and conditional on a core kill
happening the chance it is ours rises **29% → 55% → 72% → 76%** across r0-150 /
r151-300 / r301-600 / r601-999.

**Four questions.** (A) What raised the share of decisive games elsewhere? (B) What
separates a bot that CONVERTS from one that GRINDS? (C) How did anyone decide whether
to try to finish at all versus bank the tiebreak? (D) Did organisers change rules or
maps to raise decisiveness?

**Coverage.** All **22 official Battlecode postmortems 2019-2026** downloaded and
machine-searched by me. Three parallel discovery legs (Halite/Lux; CodinGame /
Terminal / Screeps / Planet Wars; StarCraft AI theory and bot source) returned
candidate strings which **I re-verified myself, mechanically, against re-downloaded
primaries** — no string in this sweep entered on a subagent's word.

**Quote discipline.** **176 quoted strings machine-verified verbatim — 132 across the
23 tactic files and 44 in this summary — 0 unverified, 2 near-misses caught and
corrected in my own drafts.** Per the s25 method finding (14 of 18 near-misses were
compressions in the *summary*, which had never been machine-audited), **this document
was audited with the same checker as the files.** Both near-misses are recorded in the
audit section below; one of them also exists in a **pre-existing library file**.

---

## THE ONE-SENTENCE ANSWER

**An economically-correct evaluator never finishes, because the last increment of kill
progress has no economic return — and the two authors who diagnosed this fixed it with
a small, unconditional, deliberately un-economic term.** Everything else in this sweep
is a corollary or a boundary condition on that.

---

## The five sharpest findings

### 1. (A) Efficiency is what stops the kill. Two leagues, two authors, one shape.

Jay Scott's Planet Wars bot searched for the materially best move, so it would not
spend ships taking planets that produce nothing — and therefore did not end games it
had won:

> *"One tiny tweak is to add a small bonus in the move generator for captures of enemy
> planets. It makes the bot a tad more aggressive, but the real point is to allow it to
> take 0-growth enemies if nothing else beckons. It stops a tiny proportion of games
> from looking silly when the enemy survives to the end owning only a 0-growth planet
> surrounded by oddshrimp’s vast fleets."*

— `http://satirist.org/ai/planetwars/`

The identical failure in a different genre: Steamhammer's anti-air suicide units are
hard-coded not to waste themselves on floating buildings — correct cost-efficiency,
and exactly what prevented the win. Its author, on the game he could not finish:

> *"The scourge might have understood that when only floating buildings are left, they
> are good targets."*

— `http://satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/842-Steamhammer-cant-finish-the-game.html`

**Why it lands on us mechanically, not by analogy.** Killing the enemy core returns
**nothing**: no titanium delivered, no harvester, no improvement to any tiebreak key.
Under our own arithmetic an attack that does not finish is a **2.2:1 donation**. So a
bot that prices actions by return is *correct* to never commit, and will grind by
construction. That is a candidate explanation for our incidence gap that requires no
new mechanism at all — only that **nothing in our bot currently pays for a core kill.**
File: [`pay-for-the-capture-with-no-economic-return`](pay-for-the-capture-with-no-economic-return.md).

### 2. (A)+(B) The converters implement finishing as a DISCRETE MODE SWITCH that replaces the economy policy — and the documented failure is doing it as a weight.

Steamhammer has a named predicate, `bool StrategyBossZerg::enemySeemsToBeDead() const`,
which is four guard clauses over remembered state, and whose header comment reads
`// The enemy is toast, and all we have left to do is to eradicate the surviving enemy buildings.`
When it fires, the whole tech planner is short-circuited to one unit type. Its author:

> *"Finishing off the enemy just means destroying all their buildings. It sounds simple,
> but it is a sophisticated skill, and there are a lot of ways to go wrong."*

and on the game it lost:

> *"In this case, Steamhammer had the wrong unit mix; to make zerglings and ultralisks
> when all enemies were in the air was no good. The mutalisk rule should make mutalisks
> only, not mix them with other units."*

**A blended finisher produced a bot that was maxed, won every fight, and could not kill
two buildings.** Against a 2.2:1 defensive edge, blending is worse than not trying.

BC2020's field independently converged on the same structure and named it — **the
crunch** — and sized it as a **rate**, not a damage total:

> *"The idea is to build a huge economy and then spawn a horde of Drones and
> Landscapers and then crunch the enemy HQ with so many of them at the same time that
> even by killing one each turn it wouldn’t be able to stop them"*

— Java Best Waifu, BC2020

That is our library's own arithmetic arriving from the field: exceed the defender's
**per-round** removal capacity and HP totals stop mattering. The crunch is three
phases — remove the repair bodies, substitute your own onto the vacated tiles, *then*
kill — and the ordering is the claim.
Files: [`the-finisher-is-a-discrete-mode-switch`](the-finisher-is-a-discrete-mode-switch.md),
[`the-crunch-is-a-rate-race-not-a-damage-race`](the-crunch-is-a-rate-race-not-a-damage-race.md).

### 3. (B) The other converter mechanism is a STANDING ALLOCATION to the win condition, run unconditionally on the game state — and one postmortem records the incidence rise it produced.

BC2024's cout for clout: *"Flag Sniping We allocate 3 ducks to be snipers, whose sole
purpose is to harass the opponent / sneak their flag out."* BC2023's don't @ me, on the
same policy as insurance:

> *"we made sure to implement mechanics for capturing islands even if we were falling
> behind economically - it could help us eke out a win in otherwise hopeless scenarios"*

and, crucially, what the ladder looked like before anyone did it:

> *"In early rounds of development, such as sprint 1, most games were settled by
> tie-breakers, due both to the fact that most teams neglected to implement island
> capturing mechanics, and the fact that anchoring islands was not as powerful as it
> would become through later patches."*

**Decisiveness rose across one season, and the stated first cause is that teams wrote
the win-condition code at all.** (The second cause is an organiser buff — question D.)
File: [`a-standing-allocation-to-the-win-condition`](a-standing-allocation-to-the-win-condition.md).

### 4. (C) Battlecode is a clean measured negative; bot SOURCE CODE is not — and this CORRECTS sweep 15.

**The Battlecode negative is machine-countable.** Across all 22 postmortems, `expected
value`, `expected win`, `win probability`, `probability of winning`, `cost-benefit`,
`worth attacking`, `abort`, `call off`, `retreat if`, `play for the tiebreak`, `bank
the`, `secure the win`, `if we are winning`, `if we are losing`, `when ahead`, `when
behind`, `losing position`, `convert the lead` and `close the game` all return **zero
files, zero hits.** The three near-misses (`settle for` ×1, `winning position` ×2,
`utility` ×6) were read in context and none is a decision rule. **The question is not
answered badly. It is not asked.**

**But outside Battlecode prose the rules exist, in source:**
- **CommandCenter** — an absolute own-unit count, latched:
  `"AttackCondition"   : [ ["Self", "Zealot"], ">=", [ 3 ] ]`
- **UAlbertaBot** — the sign of a simulated outcome and nothing else:
  `bool retreat = score < 0;`
- **PurpleWave** — a normalised force differential against a **moving** threshold:
  `val shouldFight         : Boolean = scoreTotal  >= scoreTarget`, with
  `lazy val scoreSkim = Maff.nanToOne((skimUs - skimEnemy) / (skimUs + skimEnemy))`,
  and a hysteresis block that **lowers the bar once already fighting**
  (`amount  -= .25  * entanglement`) and **raises it while idle**
  (`amount  += .10  * staticThreat`).

**THE CORRECTION.** Sweep 15 recorded as standing context that *"`KILL_WINDOW_RND: 250`
is a round number, and NO winning bot in this sweep branches on a round number"*, and
concluded that a commitment *"should probably not be a clock."* **A contest winner
branches on two bare turn numbers.** robostac, 1st in Code Royale (200 turns):

> *"- For the last 40 turns spend gold as fast as possible - for every idle barracks if
> I can afford to train then train."*

> *"- For the first 50 turns just send knights from the closest barracks to the enemy
> queen whenever possible."*

The narrower statement that survives both sweeps: **a clock is a poor ARMING trigger
and a good DISARMING/DEADLINE trigger.** Every arming trigger the library holds is on
an achievement, a count, a resource threshold or geometry; every clock it holds fires
to *stop hoarding* or *stop reacting* — a state with no natural exit. The library was
conflating the two jobs.

**And one residual negative survives every leg:** nobody, in any league or any codebase
read here, wrote a rule in the *other* direction — an explicit gate that **stops attacking and
plays for the tiebreak score**. Every positive decides whether to start or
continue a fight. That residual is the one that bears on us, because the tiebreak is
the road we currently win.
Files: [`nobody-computes-whether-to-finish`](nobody-computes-whether-to-finish.md),
[`commit-on-a-deadline-not-on-a-standing`](commit-on-a-deadline-not-on-a-standing.md),
[`the-commit-threshold-moves-once-you-are-engaged`](the-commit-threshold-moves-once-you-are-engaged.md),
[`if-the-push-fails-fall-back-to-the-clock`](if-the-push-fails-fall-back-to-the-clock.md).

### 5. (D) Designers legislate the endgame — and the lever is the MAP, never a shorter clock. Two leagues say the clock lever backfires.

**The extreme case: BC2020 deleted the score fallback entirely.** Java Best Waifu:
*"it wins if and only if the enemy HQ gets destroyed"*, and *"The water increase is
slow but exponential, which guarantees that the entire map will eventually flood (and
therefore that the game will eventually end)."* Decisiveness was **unity by
construction** — and that is the season the field invented the crunch.

**The lever they actually reach for is the map.** Three Battlecode teams, two seasons
five years apart, all describe organisers steering offence/defence via the tournament
map pool: *"the devs indirectly nerfed rush through large and hard-to-path maps in the
seeding tournament"* (The High Ground), *"Teh devs had put pretty anti-rush maps as
well."* (Confused), and as a standing pattern, *"Teh Devs have historically shown
favoritism to teams that prioritize economy-based gameplans (as opposed to rush/attack
based gameplans) by making maps in the finals tournament larger and slower."* (The Kragle). Halite III's organisers did the
same thing in the same direction, at pinned commits: the **80×80 map was deleted**
(`32x32, 48x48, 64x64, or 80x80` → `32x32, 40x40, 48x48, 56x56, or 64x64`) while the
**minimum game length ROSE from 300 to 400 turns.** More turns, smaller board — the
lever was **contact density**, not urgency.

**And the clock lever is documented going the wrong way.** BC2021 halved its round
limit mid-season (*"The game consisted of 3000 rounds (later changed to 1500 by Teh
Devs)"*), and a top competitor records the effect on a plan aimed at the *kill*:
*"this would make my strategy non-viable since the slow exponential growth technique
which I required would not produce enough conviction in time for me to be able to end
the game by extermination."* **A shorter clock made the score road the realistic one.**
This is the guardrail against reading `KILL_WINDOW_RND: 250` as "do the same thing
sooner": the field's one natural experiment says the thing being accelerated does not
compress, it just fails earlier.

**Not everyone wants decisiveness.** Halite II's community proposed forced-elimination
timers with concrete numbers — *"Loss triggered after not controlling any planet for X
turns. (proposed X = 40)"* — and an organiser closed the tracking issue with
*"I veto any change related to this. Survival is part of the meta"*. **Two leagues,
opposite decisions, both documented.** Our ruleset sits on Halite's side.
Files: [`a-rising-hazard-makes-every-game-decisive`](a-rising-hazard-makes-every-game-decisive.md),
[`the-designers-lever-was-the-map-pool`](the-designers-lever-was-the-map-pool.md),
[`contact-density-not-a-shorter-clock`](contact-density-not-a-shorter-clock.md),
[`shortening-the-clock-suppressed-the-kill`](shortening-the-clock-suppressed-the-kill.md),
[`the-organisers-refused-to-force-elimination`](the-organisers-refused-to-force-elimination.md).

## The finding that argues AGAINST the programme, filed rather than buried

**Our tiebreak pays for economy. Theirs pays for destruction.** SSCAIT's fallback score
is *"the sum of units killed plus buildings razed"*, and its rules include an explicit
anti-stalemate cut: *"The game ends automatically either after 90 in-game minutes
(86400 frames) or when no unit dies for 5 real-world minutes."* Ours is titanium
delivered → harvesters alive → titanium stored: **not one key rewards damage done.**
So our ruleset does not merely permit grinding, it *pays* for it.

And grinding is a documented top-tier strategy. Jay Scott on Slin, **3rd in Planet
Wars 2010**: *"Slin does not want to wipe you out. Slin wants to get solidly ahead and
lock you in a wrestling hold that you can’t break, and then it is content to wait for
the game to run out."* Agade, **3rd in Code Royale**: *"At the higher levels a lot of
games were won simply by having 1 more HP than the opponent"*. And the obvious fix is
reported to have failed: *"Curiously, my tries to improve oddshrimp by making it aware
of when it is ahead or behind have never come to much."*

**Consequence for how we test.** Jay Scott's SSCAIT score analysis found
*"Krasi0 was ahead in points, on average, in the games that it lost."* Every natural
proxy for an offensive change — damage dealt, siege duration, forward turrets placed —
is Krasi0's metric. **Any incidence experiment must report win rate, `core_kill_share`
AND r1000 win rate together.** If the third falls while the second rises, we have
traded our best road for our showiest one.
Files: [`the-grinder-is-a-legitimate-strategy`](the-grinder-is-a-legitimate-strategy.md),
[`ahead-on-points-in-the-games-it-lost`](ahead-on-points-in-the-games-it-lost.md),
[`organisers-legislate-the-endgame-and-name-the-incentive`](organisers-legislate-the-endgame-and-name-the-incentive.md).

## The cheapest measurement in this sweep, and it gates most of the rest

**Our r1000 tiebreak win rate, cut by opponent rating band.** If 57.2% holds at ≥1900,
the tiebreak is a genuine second road, the abort rule is worth building, and the
programme's kill-first framing is a choice. If it collapses, the score road is
worthless against the opponents who decide our rating and the incidence framing is
vindicated. **It has never been cut this way.** Three files in this sweep are
explicitly conditional on it.

Two more corpus cuts, no bot change, queued by this sweep:
1. **Core-kill incidence by map AREA and wall-tile fraction** — three independent
   design teams treated map geometry as the live lever, and BC2023's 4 Musketeers
   report a map class at *zero* decisiveness (*"There were far too many maps where
   nothing happened for 2000 rounds."*). If a zero-incidence class exists for us, every
   incidence A/B must be blocked on map class or it measures the draw.
2. **Which tiebreak key actually decided our 353 r1000 games** — key 1 (delivered),
   key 2 (harvesters), key 3 (stored), or the coinflip.

## `transfers: no` results, filed so nobody chases them

- **Church lightning.** BC2019's named stalemate-breaker was a build-chain exploiting
  *"as newly completed units had the next turn and could thus go right after the
  preceding unit"*. **Our engine is
  measured to forbid exactly that** — a unit created mid-round does not act that round
  (24,045 new entities, 0 acted), and ids are ascending so a new entity acts *last*.
  A builder cannot build a builder either.
  [`church-lightning-is-blocked-by-our-turn-order`](church-lightning-is-blocked-by-our-turn-order.md)
- **The rising-hazard design.** We cannot delete our score fallback. Filed for the
  corollary it forces: a non-decisive game is engine-sanctioned here in a way it
  structurally was not in BC2020.
- **Halite III's ship capture** — a conversion mechanic that was written into the beta
  rules (*"A ship can be captured and change owners if it is flanked by an opposing
  player’s ships."*) and **deleted three days later** with no recorded rationale.

## Reconciled against the s26 engine corrections landed mid-sweep

Two engine facts changed under this sweep while it was being written, and every
affected file was corrected in place rather than left to rot:

1. **Cost scale is ONE GLOBAL ADDITIVE TEAM FACTOR, not per-category.** Four files
   here priced a standing offensive allocation as if builder scaling touched only
   builders. It does not — **every builder we buy for a crunch raises the price of the
   turrets that crunch needs.** That makes the "standing but cheap" qualification in
   [`a-standing-allocation-to-the-win-condition`](a-standing-allocation-to-the-win-condition.md)
   *more* load-bearing, not less, and it sharpens
   [`pay-for-the-capture-with-no-economic-return`](pay-for-the-capture-with-no-economic-return.md)'s
   warning that Jay Scott's "small bonus" is not small at our prices.
2. **The sentinel is cheaper per damage than the gunner in every rating band**, and
   the gunner:sentinel price ratio is pinned 2:3 at every scale. That independently
   confirms this sweep's placement recommendation from the other direction: a crunch
   built of gunners is blinded by its own clearance bodies *and* is the dearer weapon.
   See [`optimise-the-win-condition-action-itself`](optimise-the-win-condition-action-itself.md).

## Audit — the two near-misses I caught in MY OWN drafts

Both are the families the INDEX names, and finding them in my own text is the point of
running the mechanical check on the sweep's own output.

1. **A true quote with the wrong first word.** I drafted the library's heal-cap line
   beginning **the** defender's heal is adjacency-capped. INDEX.md begins that clause
   **our** defender's heal is adjacency-capped. Corrected. **⚠ THE SAME NEAR-MISS EXISTS IN A
   PRE-EXISTING FILE:** `the-defenders-reserve-and-what-defeats-it.md` also renders that clause with **the** rather than **our**. I did not edit that file (this sweep writes new files
   only) — **flagging it for correction.**
2. **A glyph error in a title.** I drafted Steamhammer's post title with an ASCII
   apostrophe; the page uses a curly one and the ASCII form appears **nowhere** on it.
   Corrected, and the glyph noted inline.

**Method notes for the next sweep.** Two artifacts of the auditor itself, worth knowing
because both produce false FAILs: text sitting *between two adjacent inline code spans*
gets captured as if it were quoted; and **two blockquoted quotes with no blank `>` line
between them are concatenated** into one string that will never match. The second one
matters — it is indistinguishable from a real failure until you look.
