# Sweep 18 — how anyone REPRESENTED and EXECUTED a plan longer than one step

**Aimed at** the project lead's oldest unanswered ask — quoted from the sweep brief, not from
any external source: *"what i would like to investigate is bigger plans than that, more steps
that might make a bad tactic actually a good tactic"* — and at sweep 17A's structural finding
that **an economically-correct evaluator never finishes**, because every step of a successful
assault has a negative immediate return. These are one problem from two ends: a single-step
evaluator rejects every locally-bad action, and multi-step composition is the only structure
that redeems one. **The library had 17 swept topics and not one about plans.**

**Four sub-questions.** (A) What was a multi-step plan stored *in*, and does it PERSIST or is
it RE-DERIVED? (B) What made a unit stick to a locally-bad step, and what made it abandon
one? (C) Did planning beat reactive play, and did anyone MEASURE it? (D) Where did planning
LOSE?

**Coverage.** Four discovery legs (StarCraft bot source + Jay Scott's blog; Screeps +
Battlecode; RoboCup + MicroRTS; Halite/Lux/CodinGame/Planet Wars), all `model: opus`. **Every
string in this sweep was re-verified by me against a primary I downloaded myself** — the 25
tactic files carry **292 quoted spans, of which 288 verify verbatim across 61 distinct primary
documents.** The 4 that do not are **explicitly labelled internal quotations** — three of the
project lead's ask from the brief, one of `2026-08-09-sweep-17a.md` — and are marked as such
inline. **This summary was audited separately with the same checker** (61 spans, 59 verified,
the 2 misses being the same labelled internal quotations), per the s25 finding that 14 of 18
near-misses lived in an unaudited summary.

---

## THE ONE-PARAGRAPH ANSWER

**A multi-step plan wins as a COORDINATION device between agents and loses as a SUBSTITUTE for
one agent's move choice — and the two literatures that measured it disagree only because they
measured different objects.** RoboCup ablated a library of pre-stored multi-agent plans and it
won 28 games to 5 over 38 with everything else held rigid. MicroRTS ran a hierarchical-task-
network planner against hand-coded reactive scripts and the planner lost 60 of 60 games to the
simplest one. **The transferable form for us is neither a planner nor a mode flag: it is a
small table of pre-authored modes living in the source file that every unit already shares,
with a store slot carrying only an INDEX into it, each mode carrying a trigger, a behaviour and
its own termination condition.** The project lead's framing — *a committed mode with a cheap
abort predicate rather than a plan representation* — is very close to right, and the one place
it needs correcting is that **the field's cheap abort predicate is not one predicate but three
different ones doing three different jobs**, and it needs correcting *loudly* on (C).

## (A) REPRESENTATION — persist or re-derive

**The modal winning answer is RE-DERIVE, and it is not close.** Halite III's 1st, 6th and 7th
places all recompute a score for every ship × square every turn and store nothing; Lux S1's
winner is a single stateless network emitting all commands simultaneously; RoboCup's Gliders
re-scores every feasible action against a per-action goal state so **nothing is ever adopted
and therefore nothing can be abandoned**; PurpleWave clears and rebuilds its whole production
timeline every planning tick. File:
[`the-modal-winning-representation-is-no-stored-plan`](the-modal-winning-representation-is-no-stored-plan.md).

**What substitutes for commitment is a SHAPED SCORE, and the Halite III winner published the
argument.** teccles proves his target scoring is regret-free: *"If nothing changes with enemies
between these turns, its new target is usually (but not always) be T'. However, it will always
be true that X' is closer to T' than X - so even if we've changed target, we don't regret the
previous move."* **A step toward an abandoned target is still a step toward the new one.** That
is plan-like behaviour with zero storage, and it costs us nothing to check whether our own seat
scoring has the property. File:
[`shape-the-score-so-a-re-derived-choice-never-regrets-the-last-one`](shape-the-score-so-a-re-derived-choice-never-regrets-the-last-one.md).

**THE ANSWER FOR OUR 16 INTS IS 27 YEARS OLD AND IT IS EXACT.** Stone & Veloso's paper is
titled *"Task Decomposition, Dynamic Role Assignment, and Low-Bandwidth Communication for
Real-Time Strategic Teamwork"* and solves our constraint set rather than an analogue of it. Its
own contributions list names the object: *"a method for using roles to define pre-compiled
multi-step, multi-agent plans"*. The mechanism is that **the plan is never communicated** —
*"The set-plays are defined in the locker-room agreement so that they are known to all agents
on the team."* — and the runtime channel carries `<Formation-number>` and
`<Formation-set-time>`, i.e. **an index and a timestamp.** Their stated problem is ours
verbatim: *"since the agents are autonomous and do not share memory, they could easily become
uncoordinated"*.

**Our version of the locker-room agreement is FREE**: every unit runs the same `Player` class
from the same `main.py`, so a module-level table of modes is already shared by every unit at
zero bandwidth with zero latency and no possibility of disagreement about its contents. **The
store then carries one small nonnegative integer, and every measured hazard of our store —
last-writer-wins, the one-round buffer, the negative-write raise — becomes harmless.** Files:
[`the-plan-lives-in-the-code-and-the-store-carries-its-index`](the-plan-lives-in-the-code-and-the-store-carries-its-index.md),
[`one-writer-names-the-mode-and-the-rest-obey`](one-writer-names-the-mode-and-the-rest-obey.md).

**And the plan must name ROLES, not units.** *"set-play roles are not assigned to
pre-determined agents; instead they are filled by whichever agent is filling the appropriate
role in the team"*'s current formation. Stardust does the same thing as a greedy per-frame
reassignment (*"They greedily take the reassignable units closest to where they want them"*);
UAlbertaBot does it with a monotone ratchet (*"// make sure strictly less than so we don't
reassign to the same squad etc"*) evaluated only every tenth frame. **Our entity ids are
useless as names — they come from one global counter shared with resource stacks, so magnitude
is meaningless and only ordering carries information — and our raiders' survival falls to 6
rounds at r150, so any plan naming a specific builder has a 6-round lifetime.** File:
[`the-plan-names-a-role-not-a-unit`](the-plan-names-a-role-not-a-unit.md).

**Two representation corrections worth carrying.**
1. **Nobody uses behaviour trees and nobody uses a blackboard as a memory.** `behavior tree`,
   `behaviour tree`, `blackboard` and `potential field` return **zero hits across the 12
   Battlecode-family documents I downloaded myself**, and the same across Screeps and the
   StarCraft codebases per the legs. The one blackboard in the field — PurpleWave's — is
   **wiped every planning tick on purpose**. **That is exactly what our 16-slot store is, and
   the design rule falls out: separate "what I decided this round" (the bus) from "what I am
   committed to" (the committing object).** File:
   [`the-blackboard-is-a-one-tick-bus-not-a-memory`](the-blackboard-is-a-one-tick-bus-not-a-memory.md).
2. **A queue is a representation with a known disease.** UAlbertaBot's build queue gives every
   step one bit — *"bool blocking = false; // whether or not we block further items"* — and a
   non-destructive skip cursor. **The bit transfers; the queue is `partial`**, because its own
   maintainer calls head-of-line blocking the primary bug surface of the family. File:
   [`the-plan-is-a-queue-and-each-step-carries-one-bit`](the-plan-is-a-queue-and-each-step-carries-one-bit.md).

## (B) COMMIT AND ABORT — the cheap abort predicate is THREE predicates

The brief asked for *a cheap abort predicate*. The field has three, they do different jobs, and
**a bot with only one of them has a named failure mode.**

**1. VALIDITY — is the step still legal and still worth doing?** Overmind splits it in two:
*"Test every tick to see if task is still valid"* (`isValidTask` — about the actor) and
*"Test every tick to see if target is still valid"* (`isValidTarget` — about the world), each
a one-liner, with the dispatcher popping to the parent on failure. Stone's set-plays carry the
same field formally: *"A termination condition indicating the set of states in which an agent
should cease filling the set-play role and resume its normal behavior."* Tambe's STEAM states
it as three exits — *"achieved, unachievable or irrelevant"* — with the commitment clause
*"maintain the team activity at least until one of these conditions arises"*. Stardust puts it
on the plan object itself: `bool complete` and `std::shared_ptr<Play> transitionTo`. **Our
version is `can_build_<type>(seat, dir)`, which the library already measured is strictly
stronger than `is_tile_empty`.** File:
[`a-plan-step-carries-its-own-termination-condition`](a-plan-step-carries-its-own-termination-condition.md).

**2. PROGRESS — has anything happened lately?** Steamhammer's watchdog reasons about the plan
not at all: *"the.now() > _lastProductionFrame + Config::Macro::ProductionJamFrameLimit"* with
`int ProductionJamFrameLimit = 360;` (~15 s), firing *"// Looks very like a jam. Clear the
queue and hope for better luck next time."* Stone's set-play timeout is per role. PurpleWave's
is **state-conditional**: 45 seconds if the plan has not yet reached its paying phase, 90
absolute. **⚠ And the same author who wrote the watchdog says it is not enough:**
*"Steamhammer tries to mitigate these by timing out and clearing the queue if nothing has been
produced for too long, but that is not enough to save the game against a strong opponent."*
File: [`abandon-the-plan-on-a-progress-timeout`](abandon-the-plan-on-a-progress-timeout.md).

**3. GRANULARITY — how much should you abort?** This is the one the library did not have, and
it comes with the sweep's cleanest before/after. Steamhammer replanned from scratch on any
disruption; against an SCV rush that produced an infinite restart loop and
*"Steamhammer terran rarely gets a marine out before Stone beats it."* The fix was not a better
planner: *"But if a worker is lost in the opening, we can replace it, and try to stay as close
as possible to the original opening."* — after which *"Steamhammer protoss now defeats Stone
smoothly."* **Cheap loss → patch the plan; expensive loss → drop it**, and the classification
survives in shipped code. **Our cost scale makes the restart loop worse than his: it is one
global additive team factor, so a plan that restarts and re-buys is inflationary, not merely
slow.** File:
[`classify-the-disruption-before-you-replan`](classify-the-disruption-before-you-replan.md).

**AND THE COMMITMENT DEVICE IS ONE CONSTANT, SEVEN TIMES OVER.** Not a state machine — a
constant added to whatever you chose last time. Steamhammer's tech choice recomputes every
score and then, under a comment reading *"// Hysteresis."*, does
`techScores[int(_techTarget)] += 13;` in ZvT, `+= 11` in ZvP, `+= 4` in ZvZ — **commitment
strength tuned to what switching costs.** PurpleWave charges challengers a 1.5× multiplier
against incumbents. UAlbertaBot locks out retreat→attack for 100 frames and leaves
attack→retreat instant. Stardust refuses to re-evaluate a belief for 120 frames
(*"// Only change our minds after at least 5 seconds"*). Steamhammer widens the *exit* radius
by 10 tiles over the *entry* radius. TheDuck314 uses a bare 3× multiplier; mlomb a 970/300
band; TooAngel a comment that says *"// Use hysteresis to prevent ping-pong:"*. **This is the
highest evidence-to-implementation-cost ratio in the sweep and it needs no store slot.** File:
[`add-a-constant-to-the-incumbents-score`](add-a-constant-to-the-incumbents-score.md).

**Three more (B) mechanisms, each one line:**
- **A resumable interrupt for the price of one variable.** Battlecode 2026's 3MiceWalkIntoABar
  have `goToState` (updates `state` *and* `previousState`) and `goToStateTemporary` (updates
  only `state`). **That single difference is the whole "abort without forgetting" mechanism** —
  and it is the cheap alternative to the goal stack the library already holds, because our
  interrupts do not nest. Our own probe measured the interrupt it would fix: **a builder next
  to a damaged core moves in 15.5% of rounds against 68.3% at full HP, n = 143,812.** File:
  [`one-line-of-interrupt-that-remembers-what-it-interrupted`](one-line-of-interrupt-that-remembers-what-it-interrupted.md).
- **Replan only when idle.** Screeps names it: *"Target locking is a CPU saving technique where
  you cache the most optimal target for an object to move to or perform any action on,
  amortizing the cost of the selection process over a longer period of time."* Overmind's gate
  is one line — `get isIdle(): boolean { return !this.task || !this.task.isValid(); }` — with
  exactly one bypass (flee). **This is the affordable answer to our 10 ms budget**, and note
  the motivation the wiki states is CPU, not decision quality. File:
  [`replan-only-when-the-unit-is-idle`](replan-only-when-the-unit-is-idle.md).
- **When a plan dies, say where its units go.** Stardust makes the handback part of the plan's
  interface; PurpleWave decides what happens to the ones that cannot get home
  (*"// Godspeed you poor souls"*) and handles sunk cost in one line —
  *"_queueNext ++= _queueLast.view.filter(_.hasSpent)"*. **Our version of that guard is
  unambiguous: `destroy()` refunds nothing and the scale contribution persists, so titanium
  already committed must never be abandoned by a re-derivation.** File:
  [`when-the-plan-aborts-say-where-its-units-go`](when-the-plan-aborts-say-where-its-units-go.md).

## (C) ⚠ THE SURPRISE — THE ABLATION EXISTS, AND THE BRIEF SAID IT WOULD NOT

**The brief said: "A published comparison of a planning bot against its own reactive ablation
is the single most valuable thing you could find. Expect not to find one. A clean negative here
is a real result."**

**It was found, and it is reported here as the surprise it is rather than softened.** RoboCup —
the row the sweep brief flagged as **explicitly not yet swept by this library, a real gap** —
has one, with
populations, published in *Artificial Intelligence* in 1999.

Stone & Veloso state the control: *"we are able to isolate the effects of each contribution
through controlled testing"*, and *"with rigid positions and no set-plays. The behaviors of the
players on the two teams are otherwise identical."*

| leg (38 games of 10 min each) | games won | total goals | avg goals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible positions **and** set-plays | **34** (Default 1, 3 ties) | 223 (82) | 5.87 (2.16) |
| **Only set-plays** — the plan-library ablation | **28** (Default 5) | **187** (108) | **4.92** (2.84) |
| Only flexible positions | 26 (Default 6) | 157 (87) | 4.13 (2.29) |

*"The flexible team won 34 out of 38 games with 3 ties."* and *"Both characteristics provide a
significant advantage over the default team, but they perform even better in combination."*
**The middle row is the one that matters: positions rigid on both sides, the only difference
being whether the team carries a library of pre-stored multi-step multi-agent plans.**

They also entered their own ablation in a live tournament as a separate team — FCMellon,
*"identical to CMUnited except that it did not use a flexible teamwork structure: players did
not switch positions, did not use flexible positioning of any sort, and did not use
set-plays."* FCMellon had won its previous four games 49–4. CMUnited beat it **6–0**.

**THE BOUNDARY, and it must travel with the number: the ablated baseline is RIGID-SCRIPTED, not
reactive.** Nobody ablated against a memoryless reactive agent. The measured claim is *stored
multi-step plans beat no stored plans, roles held constant*, not *deliberation beats reaction*.

**And the same authors wrote the methodological sentence this library has been circling for
four sweeps:** *"Since competitions are not controlled experiments, their results are not
presented as scientific validation of our individual techniques."*

**What survives of the library's standing bound.** Sweep 15's negative was about **game
competitions**, and it holds: across the Battlecode corpus this sweep's leg found **zero** cases
of a team building a planner and its own reactive ablation and reporting the delta; the
comparisons that exist are always new-bot-vs-old-bot, which confounds the planning change with
everything else in the submission. **What changes is that an adjacent literature does run
ablations and publishes the populations — so the next sweep needing a controlled comparison
should look at RoboCup first, not last.** File:
[`set-plays-were-ablated-and-set-plays-won`](set-plays-were-ablated-and-set-plays-won.md).

**One more (C) datapoint, and it is about our testing rather than our planning.** Battlecode
2026's 3MiceWalkIntoABar built a distributed tester and gated merges on it: *"We made sure that
before we merged any changes, the tester gave us confidence that the change improved the win
rate. We used a 95% confidence interval on the win rates to make this decision."* — because
*"there was often enough local variation to make a bad change look as if it improved the bot"*.
And the 2025 champion did the opposite and won anyway: *"We briefly experimented with the idea
of using moppers to transfer paint to dying soldiers, known as our rez (resurrection) state,
but in our AB testing this seemed to do way worse so we scrapped the idea."* — then shipped it
in the final tournament on belief. **Both patterns are already in the library; this is
independent corroboration of each.**

## (D) THE NEGATIVE — hunted actively, and it is the largest section

**1. A tournament-winning bot deleted its plan representation, and its win rate did not move.**
PurpleWave, two commits one day apart: `Deleted declarative gameplans!` (**124 additions,
6,726 deletions, 82 files removed**) and `Deleted declarative gameplan DSL!` (**399 / 1,870,
139 files removed**), plus `Removed prioritization from scheduling. Removed priority from build
order logic.` the same day. **Total: 523 additions against 8,596 deletions, 221 files removed**
— including the plan combinators `If.scala`, `FlipIf.scala`, `Parallel.scala`,
`Trigger.scala`. He had *added* the declarative syntax two years earlier
(`Added new declarative syntax for strategies`, 2022-09-14). Machine-parsed from the
organisers' own results files: **AIIDE 2024 (data timestamped hours BEFORE the first deletion
commit): 2nd of 10, 82.29%, 2,970/3,609. AIIDE 2025: 3rd of 11, 82.95%, 2,826/3,407.** The rank
change is accounted for by Stardust improving 74.19% → 83.71%.
**⚠ And the public architecture document that describes his hierarchical task network has
exactly ONE commit in the repository's history, dated 2017-04-10 — it has been stale for seven
years.** This sweep nearly filed it as live architecture. File:
[`a-tournament-winning-bot-deleted-its-plan-representation`](a-tournament-winning-bot-deleted-its-plan-representation.md).

**2. THE STRONGEST NEGATIVE — the HTN planner lost 60–0 to a hand-coded rush.** MicroRTS,
10-agent round robin, *"60 different starting positions per match-up"*. Average win rates:
LightRush **55.3**, HeavyRush **52.4**, RangedRush **33.4**, **AHTN-P (minimax + HTN planning)
24.0**, WorkerRush 20.6, **NaïveMCTS 13.3 — last of ten.** Three separate scripts each beat the
HTN planner **100.0%** of 60 games. **Measured at 100 ms per decision cycle; we get 10 ms per
unit.** The scripted baseline `LightRushPO` also won the 2017 CIG Standard Track on open maps.
**What search DID win at is the shape worth copying: Puppet Search tops Table 3 at 65.8 against
the best script's 48.3, and its entire action space is those four scripts at one choice
point.** File:
[`the-htn-planner-lost-every-game-to-a-scripted-rush`](the-htn-planner-lost-every-game-to-a-scripted-rush.md).

**3. THE DEEPEST NEGATIVE — plan latency is structural, not a tuning problem.** Steamhammer's
maintainer on BOSS: *"BOSS also works more effectively with long build orders, which means that
bots tend to react slowly when the situation changes."* The crux: *"If you say you want an
observer, BOSS promises that by the end of the build order you will have an observer; that"*[’s
the only promise]. And he splits the complaint deliberately: *"The problem that BOSS makes poor
build orders is an implementation issue."* versus *"The problem that using BOSS makes a bot slow
to react to discoveries is a design issue. It can only be fixed by designing a different API."*
**A plan whose only contract is terminal cannot be steered by urgency.** Where he ended up:
*"Of course I intend to drop BOSS when I get that far."* File:
[`the-planners-only-promise-is-terminal-not-temporal`](the-planners-only-promise-is-terminal-not-temporal.md).

**4. Everyone who extended a plan past one step reported it did not help.** Halite III's winner:
*"I tried adding in plans about what square would be mined after the first one. These never
helped - I suspect because they weren't reliable enough."* Its 6th place: *"But I didn't come up
with anything good."* Agade, running a real beam search at 30 ms/turn, on using more than the
head of the best sequence: *"This never showed promise."* — and on spending more:
*"I found locally that giving myself more search time and beam width did not improve playing
strength."* Jay Scott: *"I tested depths up to 30 turns ahead. Total bust!"* and, across the
2010 field, *"alpha-beta was often a disimprovement, or at least difficult to make work well"*
… *"just as other contestants from #1 Gabor Melis to #350 krokokrusa found"*. **Counterweight
filed beside it: a fixed shallow 6-ply search WAS his single biggest improvement.** File:
[`every-author-who-extended-the-plan-past-one-step-said-it-did-not-help`](every-author-who-extended-the-plan-past-one-step-said-it-did-not-help.md).

**5. The plan layer's CPU cost, with a number.** Overmind's author on his own four-level
hierarchy: *"Prior to some recent changes, Overmind had never been a terribly CPU-efficient
bot. A major reason for this is its very hierarchical, object-oriented architecture, which
heavily employs classes."* and *"the caching changes have reduced CPU cost by over 40%!"*
**The cost was in CONSTRUCTION, not execution.** He also names where the abstraction breaks:
*"they aren’t the most flexible design, so they are bad for more complex scenarios where
multiple actions should be executed"* — **which is exactly the situation our stacked-tile heal
arithmetic describes.** File:
[`the-planning-layer-cost-as-much-cpu-as-executing-it`](the-planning-layer-cost-as-much-cpu-as-executing-it.md).

**6. Four teams deleted a multi-step routine and improved.** The Kragle:
*"Our robots would spend about half of their lifetime traveling back to towers and waiting
around towers for paint."* and *"Only after we started looking carefully at what other teams
were doing did we notice that no other team bothered having their bots refill on paint."*
don't @ me: *"We ended up removing the entire HQ assignment system, detached well assignment
decisions from the HQ, and placed it entirely onto the carriers."* wololo removed his relay
chain; Overmind removed its role layer. **⚠ And the Kragle is the same team that built the goal
stack the library already cites — the new context is that the refill errand is what forced it,
and they later deleted the errand instead. The cheapest fix for an interrupt that wrecks your
plans may be to delete the interrupt.** File:
[`deleting-a-multi-step-errand-was-the-improvement`](deleting-a-multi-step-errand-was-the-improvement.md).

**7. And the architecture that reconciles the whole sweep.** Battlecode 2021's wololo (7th) ran
both layers with a strict precedence: *"If there was a tie between multiple move directions
(which occured in most cases), then all of the best move directions were fed to the macro step,
which chose one of these move directions."* — **reactive picks the move; the plan only breaks
ties.** Jay Scott states the same allocation as a principle: *"Unit control, by the same general
principles, should be as reactive and knowledge-based as possible"*, and on the field:
*"Other than that, it seems to me that play is reactive: What have I seen, what do I see right
now?"* **The honest caveat is in the file: a tie-breaking plan can never redeem a locally-bad
step, which is the thing the project lead actually asked about.** File:
[`the-plan-only-breaks-ties-the-reactive-layer-left`](the-plan-only-breaks-ties-the-reactive-layer-left.md).

## Two corrections this sweep makes to standing library context

1. **17A's residual negative is corrected in existence.** 17A recorded (internal library
   quotation, from `2026-08-09-sweep-17a.md`): *"nobody, in any league or any codebase read
   here, wrote a rule in the *other* direction — an explicit gate that **stops attacking and
   plays for the tiebreak score**."* **CMUnited-97 did**: *"switching to
   an 8-2-0 formation if winning near the end of the game, or a 3-3-4 formation if losing. The
   triggers for these formation switches were defined as part of the locker-room agreement."*
   — and the same paper establishes 8-2-0 as the defensive formation. **The negative was true
   of the corpora 17A searched and is not true of RoboCup, which 17A itself listed as unswept.**
2. **`the-goal-stack-beats-the-mode-flag` gets its missing half and its cheaper alternative.**
   That file documents pushing and popping goals but records no validity test, and its own
   WHAT-WOULD-KILL-IT warns a stack that is never popped is a memory leak with extra steps.
   Overmind's `isValid()` — which pops to the parent and re-validates recursively — is the
   missing pop condition. And for our interrupt set, which does not nest,
   `goToStateTemporary` is the same benefit for one variable instead of a stack.

## The one `transfers: no`, and it is the contrast case the brief asked for

**Screeps' `Memory` is what our 16 integers cost us, and the price is worth writing down once
rather than rediscovering.** *"Each player has access to the global object Memory in which
he/she may store any information in the JSON format."* — that single facility is what every
Screeps plan representation in this sweep is built on, including Overmind's serialised
`ProtoTask` with its parent pointer and its cached target position *"in case vision is lost"*.
**Three things are simply impossible here: state that outlives a unit, state one unit writes
that another can read without a round of latency, and state that survives a match.** One thing
we get free that Screeps' author had to engineer: our `Player` object is never spontaneously
wiped. File:
[`what-the-sixteen-integers-actually-cost-us`](what-the-sixteen-integers-actually-cost-us.md).

## What this sweep does NOT license

- **It does not license a planner.** Every measured comparison of a *sequence-constructing*
  planner against reactive or scripted play, at compute budgets 10× ours, went against the
  planner.
- **It does not license the RoboCup number as a reason to attack.** Set-plays are passing
  sequences among mobile attackers in a game with no defender's edge. Ours is 2.2:1 (4.4:1 on
  a stacked core tile) and our damage is immobile, bought and unrefundable.
- **It does not answer 17A's structural question.** A mode index suspends per-step pricing for
  the mode's duration, which is the *shape* 17A said a converter needs — but nothing in this
  sweep measures whether such a mode converts games here.

## METHOD — three NEW traps, each of which produced a false FAIL in this sweep

The INDEX's procedure is extract → strip markup → decode entities → flatten whitespace
including `\f\r` → grep the literal. **It is still not sufficient, and here is what got past
it:**

1. **BLOCK-COMMENT CONTINUATION MARKERS SURVIVE WHITESPACE FLATTENING.** A JSDoc/Javadoc
   comment puts ` * ` at the start of every wrapped line, so **any quote spanning a line break
   inside a block comment fails the literal grep even though the text is correct.** Two Overmind
   doc-comment quotes failed this way and had to be published as adjacent fragments with the
   join stated. Same family as the `\f` page-break bug: a guard with a blind spot.
2. **`pdftotext` EMITS LIST-BULLET CONTROL CHARACTERS INSIDE SENTENCES.** Stone's open-problems
   list flattens to `same formation; and 3 \x0f how to ensure` — a numeral *and* a `\x0f` in the
   middle of the prose, neither removed by `[\s\f\r\v]+`. A quote spanning a list boundary can
   never match.
3. **INLINE `<code>` ELEMENTS INJECT SPACES INTO SURROUNDING PROSE.** The 3MiceWalkIntoABar
   article renders as `a previousState , which could allow…` — space before the comma —
   because tag-stripping leaves whitespace where the element was. Sentences containing
   identifiers must be quoted as fragments that stop at the code span.

**Plus one re-confirmation of the per-string glyph trap, and it bit a discovery leg.** On
`bencbartlett.github.io` a leg reported `you've` with an ASCII apostrophe; the page uses `’` and
the ASCII form appears nowhere. On `satirist.org/ai/starcraft/blog/archives/531`, Jay Scott's
own text uses curly `’` while a commenter's text on the **same page** uses ASCII `'`. Both were
caught only by re-verification.

**And a table-extraction trap that would have produced fabricated numbers.** MicroRTS Tables 3
and 4 must be read from `pdftotext -layout`. **The default extraction reorders those tables
column-wise**, producing number sequences that correspond to no row — a plausible-looking row of
real numbers attached to the wrong agent. This is the numbers-layer version of the library's
standing citation failure, and the only defence is reading the table twice with two extractors.
